Interview day, from hello to the oath

The civics test is twenty questions sitting inside a much longer meeting. Almost everyone prepares the twenty questions and walks into the meeting knowing nothing about it. This page is the meeting.

One appointment, four things at once

USCIS calls it the naturalization examination. It is not the test plus a chat, it is one legal proceeding with four parts.

  1. The officer places you under oath.
  2. The officer goes through your own N-400 answers with you, out loud.
  3. The officer administers the reading, writing and civics tests.
  4. The officer asks any further eligibility questions.

Parts 2 and 4 are also your speaking test. There is no separate speaking exam and there never was. That is the single most useful thing to understand about the day, because you are being tested from your first answer.

The two rules that protect you, and hardly anyone knows them

You may ask for any question to be repeated or rephrased. This is not a favour and it does not count against you. USCIS instructs its officers to repeat and rephrase questions until the officer is satisfied that the alien either fully understands the question or is unable to understand English. Guessing at a question you did not understand is the mistake, not asking again.

You do not need to understand every word. The standard is words in ordinary usage, and USCIS states in writing that you may make errors in pronunciation, spelling and grammar and still meet the English requirement. Noticeable errors are expected. Silence is not.

If your English is judged not good enough here, the appointment does not stop. The officer must still give you the reading, the writing and the civics test. A bad start does not cancel the parts you prepared.

The exact line between pass and fail

Reading

You read out loud. You pass if you convey the meaning of the sentence and the officer understands it. The officer stops as soon as you get one right, so your first sentence is the one that matters.

Does not fail youDoes fail you
Mispronouncing a wordLeaving out a content word
Wrong intonationSaying a different word instead of a content word
Omitting a short word such as a, the, ofPausing for a long time in the middle
A heavy accentErrors so heavy the officer cannot follow

Read all the content words. The small connecting words are forgiven. Slow down on the nouns and the verbs, and do not stop to fix a word you have already said.

Writing

The officer dictates the sentence out loud and you write it. You pass if the officer can understand what you wrote.

Does not fail youDoes fail you
A spelling mistakeWriting a different sentence
A missing capital letterAbbreviating any dictated word
Missing punctuationWriting nothing, or one or two loose words
Writing a number as 15 or as fifteenHandwriting nobody can read
The trap of the writing testNever abbreviate. USCIS states plainly that an applicant must not abbreviate any of the words, and an abbreviation is a failure of that sentence even when the meaning is obvious. If the officer dictates United States, write United States, not US, not U.S., not USA. Write Washington, not Wash. Write February, not Feb.

Spelling, on the other hand, is almost free. Errors of spelling, capitalisation and punctuation do not fail you unless the errors interfere with the meaning. Write it misspelled and legible rather than correct and unclear. The official word lists are here.

The civics stop rule, and what it should change in how you study

Twenty questions drawn from the 128, twelve correct to pass, nine wrong to fail. The part that guides written for the older test still get wrong is that the officer stops asking the moment you reach twelve correct, or nine wrong. USCIS calls this a departure from the 2020 Naturalization Civics Test. You will very often not hear twenty questions at all.

  • Your early questions count for more than your late ones. Twelve right in a row ends the test at question twelve, before the officer reaches the topics you are weakest on. Being fast and certain on what you do know is worth real points.
  • Never leave a question unanswered. Failing to respond is recorded exactly like a wrong answer, and nine of those end the test. A wrong guess costs the same as silence and sometimes wins.
  • You pass a question with a correct answer or an alternative phrasing of the correct answer. You are not reciting a script.

The four exemptions from the English test

Age and years as a permanent resident are counted at the time you file the N-400, not on the day of the interview, which is often a year later. That difference decides real cases.

If you areAnd a permanent resident forThen
50 or older when you file20 years or moreNo English test. Civics test still required, in your own language.
55 or older when you file15 years or moreNo English test. Civics test still required, in your own language.
65 or older when you file20 years or more10 questions from the 20 starred, 6 to pass.
Unable to comply because of a disabilityanyForm N-648 may exempt you from English, from civics, or from both.

The 50/20 and 55/15 exemptions remove the English test only. You still take the civics test, in the language of your choice, but then you must bring your own interpreter. Your interpreter must translate word for word with no opinion and no answer of their own, sign an interpreter oath and a privacy release, and show a government issued identity document. USCIS can disqualify an interpreter it considers a threat to the integrity of the examination, so do not bring the relative who wants to help you. Form N-648 must be completed by a licensed medical doctor, a licensed osteopathic doctor, or a licensed clinical psychologist, and you cannot fill it in yourself.

If you fail, which is not the end

  • You are rescheduled for a second examination between 60 and 90 days after the first.
  • The officer retests you only on the part you failed. Fail writing alone and the second appointment is the writing test and nothing else.
  • The officer may not reuse the test forms you already saw.
  • Fail a second time and the application is denied for failing the educational requirement.
  • Refusing to be tested, or refusing to answer a test question, is recorded as a failure. So is not turning up for the second appointment without a timely and reasonable request to move it.

The three ways the day can end

You leave with a written notice of results whatever happens, and it must say what comes next.

OutcomeWhat it means
ApprovedYour file goes to a quality review done by a different officer, then your office schedules the oath ceremony.
ContinuedUsually a Request for Evidence, generally 30 days to answer. A continuation is not a refusal.
DeniedThe written denial must reach you no later than 120 days after the interview, state the facts, cite the requirement you failed, and tell you how to request a hearing.

USCIS has 120 days from the initial interview to issue a decision. If no decision has come after 120 days you may ask a federal district court to review your application. Very few applicants know this deadline exists. Write the date of your interview down.

The week before, on one page

  1. Reread your own N-400, page by page. Every date, every address, every trip. You are about to be asked about a document you filled in months ago, under oath, and a contradiction you did not intend is still a contradiction.
  2. Gather the appointment notice, your permanent resident card, and every passport covering the years on your application.
  3. Drill the 20 starred questions first if you are 65 or over with 20 years, otherwise drill your weakest section until you are fast, because speed ends the test early.
  4. Check the four answers that depend on your state the week of the interview, not months before.
  5. Write out three sentences by hand, from dictation, with no abbreviations at all.
  6. Read the Oath of Allegiance out loud, twice.
  7. Decide now what you will say when you do not understand a question. Learn one sentence and keep it ready, could you please repeat that. You are allowed to use it as often as you need.
